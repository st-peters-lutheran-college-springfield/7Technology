Section 4

System Diagram

A system diagram is a visual map of your technology system. It shows where data comes from (inputs), what makes decisions (processing), and what the system does as a result (outputs). Engineers use these diagrams to communicate how a system works before building it.

Key Concept

Every digital system can be described as Inputs → Processing → Outputs. The micro:bit sits in the middle — it's the brain that reads sensor data and decides what to do.

For your zoo monitoring system, the diagram should show the real names of your components. Not just "sensor" — use the actual sensor type. Not just "output" — say whether it's an LED, buzzer, or data display.

Inputs
Temperature sensor
Light sensor
Button A / B
→
Processing
micro:bit
Reads sensor values
Compares to thresholds
Runs if/else logic
→
Outputs
LED matrix display
Buzzer alert
Serial data log

Your diagram doesn't need to be artistic — it needs to be labelled and clear. Use the Draw tab in OneNote, a typed block diagram, or photograph a hand-drawn version and insert it into your page.

In your diagram, make sure you include:

At least 2 named inputs · The micro:bit as the processing unit · At least 2 named outputs · Arrows showing the direction of data flow

Section 4 — Questions for your OneNote

1 What are the specific inputs your zoo monitoring system uses? Name the sensors and explain what each one detects.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Think about the physical sensors connected to your micro:bit. What does each one measure — temperature, humidity, light levels, sound? Write the sensor's proper name (e.g., DHT22, LDR) and describe what real-world condition it detects. Use the sentence starter: "Our system uses a [sensor name] to detect [what it measures]."

2 What processing steps does the micro:bit carry out? Describe what happens between receiving the sensor reading and producing an output.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Look at your MakeCode program. What does the micro:bit do with the sensor data? Does it compare it to a number (a threshold)? Does it use an if/else statement? Describe the logic in plain language, e.g., "If the temperature reading is above 28, then… otherwise…"

3 What are the outputs of your system? For each output, explain what information or action it produces and who it is for.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Outputs are the things people or other systems can observe or act on. Does your system show something on the LED matrix? Play a sound? Log data? For each output, ask yourself: Who notices this? What does it tell them? When does it happen? Use technical language — "LED matrix", "buzzer", "serial output".

Section 5

System Operation

System operation describes what your system does in different situations. For a zoo monitoring system, there are two key states: when conditions are safe for the animals, and when conditions become unsafe. You also need to explain how a human — the zookeeper — uses the system's information to make decisions.

✅ Safe Conditions
  • Sensor readings are within the safe range
  • micro:bit displays a normal status (e.g., green LED)
  • No alert is triggered
  • Zookeeper can confirm enclosure is normal remotely
⚠ Unsafe Conditions
  • Sensor reading crosses a threshold value
  • micro:bit detects the threshold breach in its logic
  • Alert output is triggered (flashing LED, buzzer)
  • Zookeeper is alerted and must decide how to respond
Important — The System Assists, Not Decides

Your system does not make decisions. It collects data and presents it. The zookeeper is the professional who interprets that information and takes action. Make sure your explanation reflects this — the system supports human judgement, it does not replace it.

Use specific values when you write about safe and unsafe conditions. Instead of "when it gets too hot", write "when the temperature sensor reads above 28°C". Specific values show technical understanding and make your explanation more credible.

Section 5 — Questions for your OneNote

5 What does safe look like for your system? Describe the sensor values and what your micro:bit displays when conditions are normal.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Think about what "normal" looks like for the animal's enclosure. What temperature range is safe? What does your code treat as acceptable? Describe the specific numbers, then describe what the micro:bit shows (e.g., LED pattern, no buzzer). Try this sentence structure: "When the [sensor] reads between [value] and [value], the micro:bit [does what] and the zookeeper sees/hears [what]."

6 What triggers an unsafe alert? Describe the threshold value, the sensor that detects it, and what happens to the outputs.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Look at your MakeCode code — what is the threshold number that changes what the system does? Describe the moment the reading crosses that number. What output turns on? What does the zookeeper notice? Use the word threshold in your answer. Example structure: "When the [sensor] reading exceeds [threshold value], the micro:bit triggers [output] to alert the zookeeper."

7 How does your system help the zookeeper make a decision? What information does it give them, and what might they choose to do?
💬 Prompt to help you answer

The zookeeper is a real professional — they don't just react to a beeping light. They use information to make a judgement call. What does your system tell them? Think about possible responses the zookeeper could take (e.g., investigate, adjust heating, contact a vet). Key point: make clear your system provides information — the zookeeper makes the decision. Use the phrase "supports decision-making" in your answer.

Section 6

Ethical & Wellbeing Considerations

Technology that monitors living beings raises important ethical questions. Ethics in technology means thinking about who might be affected by a system, how data is used, and whether the design respects the wellbeing of those involved. For a zoo monitoring system, the animals' welfare is the central concern.

🔕

Monitoring without disturbing

Good monitoring technology is passive — it gathers data without interfering with natural behaviour. A sensor fixed to a wall measures the environment around the animal, not the animal itself. The animal doesn't know it's being monitored, so it behaves naturally.

👤

Avoiding individual identification

Your system measures shared environmental conditions — temperature, humidity, light. It cannot tell one animal from another, and it doesn't try to. This is an important ethical boundary: environmental monitoring versus individual surveillance.

🌿

Supporting wellbeing responsibly

The goal of your system is to protect the animals by enabling faster human responses to dangerous conditions. Data collected should only be used for this purpose. Responsible use means the data isn't stored unnecessarily, shared, or repurposed.

Think about this

If you added a camera to your system to see which animal triggered the alert — how would that change the ethical picture? What new concerns would arise? You don't need to answer this here, but it shows how design choices in technology always have ethical consequences.

Section 6 — Questions for your OneNote

8 How does your system monitor the enclosure without disturbing the animals? What design choices make it non-invasive?
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Think about where your sensors are placed and what they measure. Does any part of your system require touching the animal? Does it make a noise or shine a light during normal operation? Describe why your approach is passive and respectful of the animal's space. Use words like: non-invasive, passive monitoring, environmental sensor, natural behaviour.

9 Why does your system not track or identify individual animals? Explain what it monitors instead, and why this is an important ethical boundary.
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Your sensors measure the environment (air temperature, light levels) — not the individual animal. Explain this clearly. Why is it important that animals aren't individually tracked? Think about what individual surveillance could lead to if it were extended beyond zoos (e.g., identifying, ranking, separating individuals). Key vocabulary: individual, identify, environmental data, surveillance, privacy.

10 How does your system support animal wellbeing in a responsible way? Who benefits, and how is data used only for its intended purpose?
💬 Prompt to help you answer

Explain the positive outcome your system creates for the animals — early warning, faster intervention, less unnecessary disturbance. Then think about the data itself: is it stored? Shared? Used for any other purpose? Responsible design means data is collected only for the purpose it was intended. Use the phrase "responsible use of data" in your response, and explain what that means in your context.

11 Reflection: If you could add one feature to improve animal wellbeing further, what would it be? What new ethical questions might it raise?
💬 Prompt to help you answer

This is an extension question — there's no single right answer. Suggest a real feature (e.g., adding a camera, adding a moisture sensor, logging data over time). Then think critically: what ethical issues would that new feature introduce? Would it cross the "individual identification" line? Would it store more data? This shows you can think beyond the task and apply ethical reasoning independently. Aim for 3–4 sentences.

📖 Key Vocabulary

Threshold
The specific value at which the system changes its response
Input
Data that enters the system from the real world (sensors, buttons)
Processing
The logic carried out by the micro:bit to make decisions
Output
The result the system produces (LED, buzzer, data)
Non-invasive
Monitoring that doesn't interfere with or disturb the subject
Surveillance
Ongoing observation of individuals — an ethical concern to avoid
Responsible data use
Using data only for its intended purpose, not storing or sharing unnecessarily

Technical Vocabulary Reference

System diagram
A labelled visual showing inputs, processing, and outputs
Sensor
A device that detects real-world conditions and converts them to data
micro:bit
The programmable microcontroller that acts as the processing unit
Threshold
A boundary value that triggers a change in system behaviour
LED matrix
The 5×5 grid of lights on the micro:bit used as a visual output
Serial output
Data sent from the micro:bit to a connected device for logging
Non-invasive
Monitoring that doesn't require contact with or disturbance of the subject
Environmental monitoring
Measuring the conditions around living things, not the beings themselves
Decision support
Information provided to help a human make a better-informed choice
Ethical consideration
A question about the impact and fairness of a technology's design or use
Animal wellbeing
The physical and psychological health and comfort of animals in care
Responsible data use
Using data only for its stated purpose; not oversharing or over-storing
